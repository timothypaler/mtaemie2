CHAPTER 01

Biochemistry & medicine

The molecular foundation of care.

+

What I learned

Chapter 1 introduced me to Biochemistry as the molecular foundation of medicine. Biochemistry studies the molecules and chemical reactions that allow living organisms to function. More importantly, it connects basic science with health, disease, diagnosis, and therapy.

What stood out to me

Medicine does not begin only when we see a patient. It begins much deeper—with molecules, reactions, enzymes, and processes occurring inside the body.

My reflection

As a future physician, I cannot only recognize what a disease looks like. I also need to understand what is happening underneath it.

Connecting the dots

Molecular changes → cellular changes → tissue or organ dysfunction → clinical manifestations → disease.

This connection helps explain laboratory findings, disease mechanisms, diagnosis, and treatment.

Core memory

“Every clinical manifestation has a story happening underneath it.”

02
CHAPTER 02

Water & pH

The balance that makes life possible.

+

What I learned

Something as simple as water is fundamental to life. I studied its unique properties and the interactions that make biological processes possible.

  • Hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions
  • Salt bridges and van der Waals forces
  • Acids, bases, pH, and pKa
  • Buffer systems and physiologic buffers

My reflection

Before this chapter, I mostly thought of water as simply the main component of the body. Now I understand that its chemical properties allow many biological processes to occur. Water provides the environment where biochemical reactions happen, while proper pH allows proteins, enzymes, and other molecules to function correctly.

Why pH matters in medicine

The body cannot allow its pH to change drastically whenever acids or bases are produced. Bicarbonate, phosphate, and proteins help maintain the conditions necessary for life.

This helped me appreciate why acid-base balance is important in conditions involving respiration, metabolism, and organ dysfunction.

Core memory

“Water enables life, but pH keeps life in balance.”

03
CHAPTER 03

Amino acids & peptides

Small structures. Significant consequences.

+

What I learned

Amino acids are much more than simple building blocks of proteins.

  • Unique R groups influence their interactions and properties.
  • They can be acidic, basic, aromatic, aliphatic, or sulfur-containing.
  • They participate in metabolic pathways and join through peptide bonds.
  • Their sequence influences the eventual properties of proteins.
  • pKa, ionization, electrical charge, peptide structure, and L- and D-amino acids describe how they behave.

Connecting structure and function

The structure of a molecule determines how it behaves. When amino acids are connected into a specific sequence, that sequence influences the structure and function of the resulting protein.

My reflection

Even a small change at the molecular level can potentially have consequences for the entire body. Diseases can arise from changes in molecules that seem extremely small but have major effects.

CHAPTER 04

Primary protein structure

Reading a protein’s first story.

+

What I learned

Proteins are not random chains of amino acids. Their primary structure is the specific amino acid sequence. I was introduced to methods used to study and analyze proteins.

  • Edman degradation
  • SDS-PAGE
  • Mass spectrometry
  • DNA cloning
  • Genomics and proteomics
  • Bioinformatics

My reflection

What surprised me was how Biochemistry connects with modern technology. We are not only learning what proteins are—we are learning how scientists identify, analyze, and understand them.

From structure to disease

Amino acid sequence → protein structure → protein function → normal physiology.

When something goes wrong: altered protein → altered function → cellular dysfunction → disease.

Core memory

“Structure gives us clues about what a protein can do.”

05
CHAPTER 05

Higher orders of protein structure

It matters how the pieces come together.

+

What I learned

  • Primary structure: the amino acid sequence.
  • Secondary structure: α-helices, β-sheets, bends, and loops.
  • Tertiary structure: the three-dimensional arrangement of a single polypeptide.
  • Quaternary structure: the association of multiple polypeptide subunits.

These structures are stabilized by hydrogen bonds, ionic and hydrophobic interactions, van der Waals forces, and disulfide bonds.

When protein folding goes wrong

A protein must fold properly to perform its function. Disrupted folding can cause loss of normal function or harmful aggregates. This helped me understand the relationship between protein misfolding and disease.

Clinical connection

The chapter introduced conditions involving abnormal protein folding, including prion diseases, as well as disorders involving collagen structure.

My reflection

The body does not only need to produce the correct protein. It also needs that protein to fold and assemble correctly.

06
CHAPTER 06

Myoglobin & hemoglobin

The chemistry behind every breath.

+

What I learned

This chapter felt especially clinically relevant to me. Structural differences explain the different functions of myoglobin and hemoglobin.

Myoglobin

  • Monomer, found primarily in muscle
  • Stores and facilitates oxygen availability
  • Hyperbolic oxygen-binding curve

Hemoglobin

  • Tetramer, found in red blood cells
  • Transports oxygen throughout the body
  • Sigmoidal oxygen-binding curve

Cooperativity and the Bohr effect

Hemoglobin shifts between the T state, with lower oxygen affinity, and the R state, with higher oxygen affinity. This allows oxygen loading in the lungs and release where it is needed.

In tissues, increased H⁺ and CO₂ promote oxygen release. Oxygen delivery depends on how the biochemical environment influences hemoglobin—not simply on having enough oxygen.

From hemoglobin to disease

  • 2,3-BPG regulation and the Bohr effect
  • CO₂ transport
  • Hemoglobin variants and sickle cell hemoglobin
  • α- and β-thalassemias

Molecular defect → abnormal protein → altered red blood cell function → impaired oxygen delivery → clinical symptoms.

My reflection

A change in protein structure or synthesis can affect something as important as oxygen delivery.

C01
CLINICAL CONNECTION / SGD

A case that challenged my first assumption

When Iron Is Not Enough

+

The SGD “When Iron Is Not Enough” involved Henry, a five-year-old boy with pallor, easy fatigability, decreased exercise tolerance, and exertional shortness of breath.

His initial laboratory findings showed anemia with low hemoglobin, hematocrit, MCV, MCH, and MCHC. His anemia persisted despite a month of oral iron supplementation.

C02
CLINICAL CONNECTION / SGD

Reading the findings together

When Iron Is Not Enough

+

At first glance, Henry’s microcytic anemia could suggest iron deficiency. Additional findings made me question that assumption.

  • Persistent microcytosis
  • Normal ferritin, serum iron, and TIBC
  • Increased reticulocyte count
  • Mildly elevated indirect bilirubin and LDH
  • Target cells and abnormal hemoglobin findings

These findings pointed toward a disorder involving hemoglobin synthesis rather than simply iron deficiency. A laboratory finding should never be interpreted in isolation.

C03
CLINICAL CONNECTION / SGD

Chapters 3–6 became one story

When Iron Is Not Enough

+

Amino acids build proteins. The amino acid sequence determines primary structure, while folding and higher-order structure influence function. A defect affecting globin synthesis can ultimately cause anemia and impaired oxygen delivery.

α-globin gene defect → reduced α-globin synthesis → globin-chain imbalance → abnormal erythropoiesis or hemolysis → reduced effective red blood cell function → reduced oxygen delivery → pallor, fatigue, exercise intolerance, and dyspnea.

The SGD required us to relate α-globin defects to globin-chain imbalance, ineffective erythropoiesis, hemolysis, reduced oxygen delivery, and reticulocytosis. Biochemistry can explain the journey from a genetic defect to a patient’s symptoms.

C04
CLINICAL CONNECTION / SGD

Family history tells a biochemical story

When Iron Is Not Enough

+

Henry’s father had significant microcytosis and anemia, while his mother had relatively normal findings.

Biochemistry is not isolated from genetics and inheritance. A molecular defect can pass through generations and produce different clinical manifestations depending on the inherited changes.

This connected with applying the inheritance pattern of α-globin gene deletions to genetic counseling.

PERSONAL REFLECTION

How my understanding changed

Looking back, connecting, and moving forward.

+

Before

Biochemistry felt like memorization. Proteins were just building blocks, hemoglobin was an oxygen-carrying protein, and anemia was mainly a CBC finding. The chapters felt separate, and I focused on passing.

Now

Biochemistry is about relationships. Protein structure determines function. Hemoglobin’s structure and regulation determine oxygen delivery. Anemia can be traced to molecular and biochemical abnormalities. The chapters are interconnected, and I am beginning to see their importance in patient care.

I am no longer asking only “What is it?” I am learning to ask, “Why does it happen?”

My PT background taught me to see function. Biochemistry is teaching me to understand what happens underneath that function.
